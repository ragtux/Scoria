# Magma viscosity

Viscosity is the single property that decides what a magma will do: flow for kilometres, fountain, froth into [[Scoria]], or freeze into a foam of [[Pumice]]. It spans more than ten orders of magnitude between basalt and rhyolite.

## The numbers

| Melt | SiO₂ | T at eruption | Viscosity (Pa·s) | Like |
| --- | --: | --: | --: | --- |
| [[Basalt]] | 50 % | 1 150 °C | 10–10² | ketchup to honey |
| [[Andesite]] | 58 % | 1 000 °C | 10³–10⁵ | peanut butter |
| [[Rhyolite]] | 73 % | 850 °C | 10⁶–10⁹ | glass at its softening point |

Water is 10⁻³ Pa·s. The gap between basalt and rhyolite is the gap between water and a glacier.

## Why silica

Silica tetrahedra link into chains and networks in the melt. More silica means more polymerisation and a melt that resists flow. Three things cut the chains:

- **Temperature.** Viscosity falls roughly a factor of ten per 100 °C.
- **Dissolved water.** Water breaks Si–O–Si bridges; 4 % water lowers a rhyolite's viscosity by four orders of magnitude. That is why a wet rhyolite can erupt as [[Pumice]] while a dry one makes [[Obsidian]] domes.
- **Alkalis.** Na and K act as network modifiers, which is why [[Mount Etna|Etna]]'s potassic basalt behaves as it does.

Two things raise it: crystals, which make the magma a suspension, and bubbles at low shear rates.

## Consequences

```
  low viscosity   →  bubbles escape or coalesce  →  fountains, scoria, long flows
  high viscosity  →  bubbles trapped             →  foam, explosion, pumice and ash
```

> [!note] Why there is no basaltic pumice
> Basalt is too runny to hold a fine foam; the bubbles coalesce and burst before the melt can freeze around them. What it can hold, at the top of a tall fountain, is the open network of [[Reticulite]].

## Measuring it

In the lab, with a concentric-cylinder viscometer on a remelted sample. In the field, from how fast a flow moves down a known slope in a channel of known depth (the Jeffreys equation), or from the thickness and spacing of pāhoehoe ropes.
