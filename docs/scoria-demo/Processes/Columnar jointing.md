# Columnar jointing

A thick lava flow cools from its top and bottom inward. As it cools it shrinks, and because the surface is held by the already-solid rock around it the shrinkage opens cracks. The cracks grow inward perpendicular to the cooling surface, spacing themselves to relieve the stress, and the result is a set of polygonal columns.

![[giants-causeway.jpg|600]]
*The Giant's Causeway, County Antrim: about 40 000 columns in a Paleogene basalt flow. Photo: Chmee2, CC BY 3.0.*

## Why hexagons

A crack network that relieves stress with the least crack area is hexagonal, the same reason mud cracks and foam cells tend to six sides. Real columns are five- to seven-sided because the cracks start as a random network and only approach the ideal as they propagate inward.

## Column size

| Cooling rate | Column width | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| fast | 10–30 cm | thin flows, flow margins |
| moderate | 0.5–1 m | the Giant's Causeway |
| slow | 1–3 m | thick ponded flows, sills, Devils Tower |

Slow cooling gives the cracks time to merge and the columns grow wide.

## Where

Any lava thick enough to cool slowly, so mostly [[Basalt]], which flows thick and far. The Giant's Causeway, Fingal's Cave, Devils Tower (a phonolite), the Columbia River basalts, and the Daguoye coast in Taiwan photographed in [[Basalt]].

> [!note] Not a vesicular process
> Columnar basalt is the dense, degassed core of a flow; the [[Scoria|scoriaceous]] top and bottom of the same flow do not form columns. The two textures are the same lava read at different heights.
