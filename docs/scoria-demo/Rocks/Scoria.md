# Scoria

Scoria is a dark, highly **vesicular** volcanic rock of basaltic or andesitic composition. It forms when gas-rich mafic lava is thrown out of a vent and quenches in the air with its bubbles still in it. Most of a [[Cinder cone]] is scoria, and so is the clinkery crust of an ʻaʻā flow.

![[scoria-hand-sample.jpg|480]]
*A hand sample: the vesicles are a few millimetres across and the walls between them are glassy. Photo: B. Domangue.*

## What it is

- **Composition:** basalt or basaltic andesite, 45–55 % SiO₂. It sits in the lower left of the [[TAS classification|TAS diagram]].
- **Texture:** vesicular, often with thin bubble walls; aphanitic or glassy groundmass with scattered olivine or plagioclase phenocrysts.
- **Colour:** black when fresh, red to brown where the iron in the glass has oxidised during or just after the eruption. The red cones in the [[Sunset Crater|San Francisco Volcanic Field]] are oxidised scoria.
- **Density:** 1.0 to 1.8 g/cm³ bulk. Unlike [[Pumice]] it almost always sinks, because its vesicles are larger, fewer and connected.

> [!note] Scoria versus pumice
> The two are told apart by colour and density before anything else: scoria is dark and sinks, pumice is pale and floats. The difference comes from the magma: mafic melt has a low [[Magma viscosity|viscosity]], so its bubbles grow large and coalesce, while silicic melt freezes around a foam of tiny bubbles.

## Vesicles

![[Glossary#^vesicle]]

The vesicles record the last seconds of the melt's life. Round ones mean the clast was still fluid when it landed; stretched ones mean it was deforming as it quenched. Vesicularity in scoria runs from about 30 % to 70 %, measured the way [[Vesiculation#Measuring it]] describes.

| Vesicle size | Typical in | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| < 1 mm | pumice, reticulite | fast nucleation, little growth |
| 1–5 mm | scoria | growth and some coalescence |
| > 5 mm | bombs, flow crusts | slow quench, extensive coalescence |

## Where it forms

Scoria is the product of [[Strombolian eruptions]], where the gas has separated into slugs that burst through a lava surface and throw out clots of melt. Each clot is a scoria clast by the time it lands. Cones of it build up around the vent at the angle of repose, which is why [[Sunset Crater]] and [[Parícutin]] have the same profile.

It also forms as the crust of basaltic lava flows. ʻAʻā flows carry a rubble of scoriaceous clinker on top, which is the "cinder" of the Hawaiian cinder fields.

## Field tests

1. Does it sink? Scoria does; [[Pumice]] does not.
2. Scratch it with a knife: the glass is about Mohs 5, so steel barely marks it.
3. Look at the vesicle walls with a hand lens: glassy and smooth in fresh scoria, dull and earthy once it has weathered.
4. Weigh a known volume. Anything under 1.0 g/cm³ is probably not scoria.

## Uses

Crushed scoria is sold as *lava rock*: landscaping mulch, drainage fill, lightweight concrete aggregate, barbecue briquettes, and the red roadbed material around the cinder cones of the American Southwest. Because the vesicles hold water and air it is also a good growing medium for succulents and bonsai.
